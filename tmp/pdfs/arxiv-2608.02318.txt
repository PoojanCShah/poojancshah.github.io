                                                                                              Abstract
                                              The celebrated k-means++ algorithm of Arthur and Vassilvitskii (SODA 2007) achieves an
                                             O(log k) expected approximation guarantee for the classical k-means problem using a technique
                                             called D2 -sampling, which has become ubiquitous in the design of clustering algorithms. Bhat-
                                             tacharya et al. (ESA 2020) introduced ε-noisy k-means++, in which the sampling probabilities

                                             approximation, but their analysis loses a constant factor of approximately 147,638 even as ε → 0,
                                             leaving open the possibility that k-means++ may be highly sensitive to even small amounts
                                             of adversarial noise. They asked whether one can instead obtain a bound within a 1 + O(ε)
                                             factor of the classical guarantee. We resolve this question affirmatively, proving an expected

                                             The main technical obstacle is controlling the average cost of the uncovered clusters in the
                                             potential-based framework of Dasgupta (2013). The proof goes through the analysis of a simple
                                             to state adversarial sampling game; where the goal is to obtain a tight bound on the average
                                             weights of elements as the game progresses. We overcome this obstacle with a short and elemen-
                                             tary argument by considering the complete cumulative distribution function of the weights and
                                             showing that a certain high-low statistic related to the average weight is a super-martingale.

The practical influence of this idea extends beyond its theoretical applications: for example, the
KMeans implementation in scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011; The scikit-learn developers, 2026) uses
a greedy variant of k-means++ as its default initialization. The classical analysis, however, assumes
that every D2 -sampling probability is computed and sampled exactly. This assumption is stronger
than what an implementation can generally guarantee. Squared distances, their sum, and the
resulting normalized probabilities are evaluated using finite-precision arithmetic and are therefore
subject to rounding error (Goldberg, 1991). Approximate distance computations, reduced-precision
arithmetic, and other performance-oriented implementations may introduce further perturbations.
Thus, even when the intended distribution is the D2 -distribution, the distribution used by an
implementation may only approximate it.

Motivated by this discrepancy, Bhattacharya et al. (2020) initiated the study of ε-noisy k-means++;
see Algorithm 1. At each step j, an adaptive adversary chooses a normalized distribution qj

1.1   Discussion on our techniques and related work
The analysis of noisy k-means++ in prior work usually goes through a related adversarial sampling
game the details of which we discuss in Section 2. Indeed, our improved bounds for noisy k-means++
are also a consequence of a more refined analysis of this sampling game in Section 3. A high
level description of the game is as follows: initially, there is a set of elements, with each being
assigned a non-negative weight value. The game then proceeds in successive rounds. In each round,
an adversary can either sample an element according to a distribution which is close to being
proportional to its weight and delete it from the set of elements, followed by decreasing the weights
of all surviving elements. Our goal is to study how the expected weight of the surviving elements
evolves over the rounds. Ideally, we want the average to grow in a bounded manner as compared
to the average at the start of the game. A tighter upper bound on the expected average through
the rounds with respect to the initial average corresponds to a better approximation guarantee for
Algorithm 1.

Bhattacharya et al. (2020) bound the average via a two-stage probabilistic argument - a Chernoff
bound to show that most deletions behave like proportional sampling, followed by a union bound over
all surviving elements to control the maximum surviving value, which yields a bound with a residual
O(log k) factor. Grunau et al. (2023a) instead partition elements into fixed big/medium/small
weight classes and analyze the transitions between these classes directly, which removes the log k
dependence but at the cost of an enormous, non-vanishing constant. Our key insight is to study
the cumulative distribution function of the weights as a whole instead of directly attacking the
average itself. Instead of a coarse and fixed partitioning of the weights, we consider a high-mid-low
partition at every threshold t ≥ 0 simultaneously (Definition 3.2). We then show that a high–low
ratio statistic related to the CDF is a bounded supermartingale (Lemma 3.4), a fact that follows
from one direct pairwise probability comparison (Lemma 3.3) rather than concentration inequalities
or case analysis. Integrating this one inequality over all thresholds via the layer-cake identity
(Lemma 3.5) then recovers the average cost directly. Because the argument is a martingale inequal-
ity rather than a probabilistic tail bound, it holds exactly for every k with no need for k to be
large, and because it uses a continuum of thresholds rather than a fixed partition, it avoids both
the log-factor loss of Bhattacharya et al. (2020) and the non-vanishing constant of Grunau et al.
(2023a), yielding the clean dependence that converges to the noiseless bound as ε → 0 in Theorem 3.1.

Theorems 5.1 and 5.6 clarify the role of the noise model. The first adapts the lower-bound instance
of Arthur and Vassilvitskii (2007) and proves that a 1 + Ω(ε) loss relative to exact k-means++ is
unavoidable. The second shows that pointwise multiplicative control is qualitatively essential: under
per-round total variation closeness, no finite approximation guarantee is possible even for k = 2.
It is worth noting that there are several algorithms which speed up k-means++ in practice using
approximate sampling techniques such as MCMC sampling (Bachem et al., 2016a,b) or rejection
sampling (Cohen-Addad et al., 2020; Shah et al., 2026) which usually have an additive guarantee
related to the variance of the dataset stemming from using weaker notion of closeness of distributions

2.1   Analyzing β = 1
We recall the definition properties of a supermartingale; see, for example, Williams (1991).
Definition 2.2 (Supermartingale). Let (Ω, F, P) be a probability space with a filtration (Ej )j≥0 ,
that is, an increasing sequence of σ-algebras E0 ⊆ E1 ⊆ · · · ⊆ F. A sequence of random variables
(ρj )j≥0 is a supermartingale with respect to this filtration if, for every j ≥ 0,
  (i) ρj is Ej -measurable;

 (ii) E[|ρj |] < ∞;

 (iii) E[ρj+1 | Ej ] ≤ ρj almost surely.
The sequence is bounded if there exists M < ∞ such that |ρj | ≤ M almost surely for all j.

6    Conclusion and Discussion
In this work, we studied the robustness of the k-means++ algorithm and resolved an open question
posed by Grunau et al. (2023a). It would be very interesting to see if our techniques extend to
noisy variants of other algorithms which use D2 -sampling such as greedy k-means++ (Bhattacharya
et al., 2020; Grunau et al., 2023b) or k-means∥(Bahmani et al., 2012).
